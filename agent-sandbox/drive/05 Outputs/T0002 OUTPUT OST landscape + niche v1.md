## Bottom line

Many outlets cover space history, and many cover space law. **None tells the story of the Outer Space Treaty (OST) through what newspapers printed at the time, from Sputnik in 1957 to entry into force in 1967.** Official archives such as UNOOSA's travaux préparatoires and FRUS hold the diplomats' paper trail. Space-history creators cover rockets and astronauts. Space-law outlets cover today's disputes. The gap between them is the public's view of the treaty: what readers in New York, Moscow, London, Lagos and Delhi were told about "who owns the Moon" while the treaty was being made.

**Timing is the strongest asset.** The treaty turns 60 on **27 Jan 2027** (signing, 91 days from today) and **10 Oct 2027** (entry into force). UNOOSA has proposed **UNISPACE IV in Vienna for July 2027**; the dates are still in brackets and funding is unconfirmed. A site that launches before January and builds toward October will have little competition for "OST 60" searches and press interest.

### What the landscape shows
1. **No one owns "OST history for the public."** The archives have no story, the creators have no law, and the law outlets have no history.
2. **No one uses the press as the lens.** That lens is defensible because newspapers capture public expectations and fears ("Moon land grab", "bombs in orbit"), which today's debates (Artemis Accords, space resources, ASAT tests) echo.
3. **The source base has a hole that is free to fill.** Free US scans stop in 1963, and the 1964–67 US press is paywalled and still under copyright. Non-US free archives (Papers Past, Trove, Delpher, Gallica; the last three are ⚠️ unchecked) plus short fair-use quotes from US papers make the site useful as a research guide, not just a gallery. **Assumption to check:** US newspapers from 1964 onward are under copyright, so the site should quote and cite, not republish whole pages.

**Primary niche: segment 2, with segment 3 as the influence layer.** Segments 1 and 4 bring reach. Segment 2 is the one no one else serves, it returns every term, and it turns the site's work into citations, which is the defensible asset.

## 3. Defensible niche

**"The Outer Space Treaty as the world read it": a dated, sourced and searchable record of how newspapers in many countries covered the treaty's making, 1957–1967, with each story linked to the treaty article it later shaped.**

What makes it hard to copy:
- **A curated corpus.** Each item is dated, sourced, tagged by country and tagged by OST article, so the corpus grows more valuable as it grows.
- **The link between press items and articles.** The site joins press items to the UNOOSA document symbols and FRUS documents for the same weeks. No one else has done this alignment.
- **Many countries.** Most space-history content is US-centric. Non-US sources (NZ, Australia, UK, Netherlands, France, plus the Soviet press through translations) set the site apart, and that suits a UN audience of 110 states.
- **Timing.** First mover into OST-60 (Jan and Oct 2027) and UNISPACE IV.

## 4. Positioning statement (draft)

> For students, practitioners and curious readers of space law who want to know why the Outer Space Treaty says what it says, **[site name TBD]** is the newspaper record of the treaty's making. Unlike UN document archives (complete but unreadable) and space-history channels (readable but silent on law), it shows, headline by headline, what the world was told, feared and expected about owning the Moon, putting weapons in orbit and being responsible in space, and links every story to the treaty article it became.

Tagline options: *"Before it was law, it was news."* · *"The Outer Space Treaty, as the world read it."* · *"Sixty years of 'Who owns the Moon?'"*

## 5. Five content pillars

1. **Headline → Article.** One article of the OST per piece (I–XVII). Show the 1957–67 headlines that raised the issue, then the final text, then the current dispute. Example: Article II (no national appropriation) traced through "Who owns the Moon?" coverage, then to the 2015 US Space Resource Act and the Artemis Accords.
2. **This Week in 1966: the countdown.** A weekly dispatch that follows the negotiations 60 years later, week by week, using press items and UNOOSA/FRUS documents. It runs from now to 27 Jan 2027, then on to 10 Oct 2027. This is the main cadence and newsletter engine.
3. **The World's Front Page.** Same day, many countries: how London, Moscow (in translation), Wellington, Sydney and New York covered the signing (27 Jan 1967), the US Senate vote (25 Apr 1967) and entry into force (10 Oct 1967). These are visual and shareable, and they are the hook for segment 1.
4. **Source Desk.** Research guides covering where the 1957–67 newspapers are (free versus paywalled, copyright notes) and how to cite them alongside UN document symbols. Lesson packets for teachers. This pillar earns links from law libraries and LibGuides.
5. **Then & Now.** Short essays setting 1960s fears beside 2020s events: orbital weapons and ASAT tests, the 1966 "Moon claims" panic and asteroid mining, the "astronauts as envoys" clause and commercial crews. These are pitch-ready for The Space Review and for guest spots on podcasts.
